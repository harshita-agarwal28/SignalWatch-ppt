A relative-anomaly watchlist and alert engine, built and presented by Harshita Agarwal
The Problem

A price list isn't a watchlist

Static ticker tables force every trader to manually calculate, by eye, whether a move is normal or worth acting on. That judgment is the actual product.

"Is a 2% move normal for this stock, or is it actually unusual?"
Relative to that stock's history
"Is this company news, or is the whole sector drifting?"
Checked against sector trends
"What actually changed since I last logged in?"
Remembered per account

SignalWatch answers all three automatically, for every tracked stock, before the dashboard finishes loading, and every answer is backed by auditable math.

How It Works

Four steps, three rules

The core loop running behind the scenes, built around zero guessing, full auditability, and responsible hedging.

1
Track each stock's normal baseline
2
Compare today's move to baseline
3
Filter by sector and market context
4
Broadcast only to relevant accounts

Deterministic math, no AI

Every alert is driven by plain arithmetic on real data. Nothing is inferred by a generative model, eliminating the risk of AI financial hallucinations.

Evidence-first interface

No signal is asserted without the exact metrics behind it displayed openly on the card. If you can't see the math, the claim doesn't get made.

Responsible hedging

Wording stays deliberately careful: "possible explanation," "cause unconfirmed." SignalWatch surfaces telemetry. It never gives investment advice.

Meaningful Change · Part 1 of 4

Baseline volatility, and the 1.2% floor

Why fixed alert rules like "flag anything over 2%" fail on both calm and volatile stocks.

Relative thresholding

Every move is measured dynamically against that specific stock's own typicalMove—its recent average daily swing.

The 1.2% safety floor

Effective Baseline
=
MAX( typicalMove, 1.2% )

On an ultra-flat stock, typicalMove sits near zero. Dividing by zero blows up the system on a two-cent wobble. The floor guarantees safety and suppresses micro-noise.

Meaningful Change · Part 3 of 4

Company, Sector, or Market?

Every move is checked in this order before it's allowed to say "this is about the company."

A one-stock sector, handled

If Tesla is the only Automotive stock tracked, comparing it to "its sector" naively just compares it to itself, always zero difference. The fix excludes the stock from its own comparison first; with no peers left, it falls back to the broad market check instead.

How close counts as "aligned"

A move counts as matching its sector or the market if it's within 0.6× the stock's typical move of that average. Past that, it's considered its own thing.

Persistence

The server-side cursor

Why "since you last checked" tracking lives in the SQLite database, not the browser.

Current price: Overwritten

One row per stock. Every tick replaces it. No need to store ephemeral ticks forever.

Signal events: Permanent

Every detected anomaly is permanently appended as an immutable historic fact.

The Cross-Device Flaw

If cursors live in browser LocalStorage, switching devices resets the timeline. A database cursor ensures perfect sync everywhere.

Interface Mathematics

The math driving the visual UI

Translating calculated ratios into immediate visual insights.

Live Radar

  • Distance: Today's move ÷ typical move (capped at 3×).
  • Colour: Red = High Attention, Green = Up, Yellow = Down.

Market Pulse & Absolute Magnitude

By summing the Math.abs() (absolute magnitude) of all stocks, chaos correctly registers as Heated in both market rallies and market crashes.

Noise Control

One alert, not a flood

Preventing volatile stocks from spamming the system every five seconds.

The 15-Minute Rule

A stock can only trigger one signal every 15 minutes to prevent alert fatigue, unless the severity genuinely escalates to High Attention.

Personal Read Receipts

Marking a signal as "Reviewed" writes to a personal join table. It hides the alert on your Focus Mode, but keeps it active globally for everyone else.
